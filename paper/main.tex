\title{Auditing and repairing silent failures of a controlled quantity in evolutionary optimization}{Auditing and repairing silent control-variable failures}

\author[1]{Yuxuan ZHANG}{{Zhangyuxuan@aeu.edu.cn}}

\address[1]{Graduate School, Army Engineering University of PLA, Nanjing 210007, China}

\abstract{A computational experiment is only as credible as the quantities it claims to control. We study a failure mode in which such a quantity silently stops being controlled: in reference-vector evolutionary optimization the population size comes from a lattice of rounded cardinality; where that value is written back over the request, the declared size is not the size the runs use. We give the problem a calculus. First, a fidelity principle over the requested, realised and reported sizes, with a characterisation of the audited constructor's admissible cardinalities and their deficit. Second, a monotonicity proposition fixing when cardinality alone favours an arm --- under set inclusion --- and a report that across independent runs the direction is not uniform. Third, a decision procedure that locates the offending assignments, sound with a measured residual, and a repair catalogue whose member depends on how an algorithm couples population to reference set. On a widely used platform the assignment occurs in at least 84 of 1887 files (at least 83 of 360 algorithms); on fifteen problems restoring fidelity leaves the indicator better on twelve, ten of them under an indicator-cardinality control, with four surviving Holm correction under unpaired tests.}
\keywords{reproducibility of computational experiments, controlled quantities, software audit, evolutionary optimization, benchmarking methodology, population-size fidelity}

\section{Introduction}
The credibility of a comparison between multi-objective evolutionary algorithms rests on the experimental conditions it claims to hold, and one of those conditions is quietly load-bearing: the population size. Decomposition-based and reference-vector multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) dominate current practice, and their population is organised around a set of reference vectors whose cardinality is taken to be the population size $N$~\cite{1,2,3}. The field is moving quickly on two fronts --- large language models woven into the evolutionary loop~\cite{4,5,6} and large-scale and many-objective problems~\cite{7,8} --- and across both one quantity is quietly load-bearing: the population size. It fixes the number of reference vectors and therefore the resolution of the search, it fixes the evaluation budget consumed per generation and therefore how many generations fit into a fixed budget, and it is reported in every experimental section as a controlled quantity. Whether a comparison is fair hinges on it, a point made explicitly in recent community guidance on fair benchmarking of evolutionary multi-objective algorithms, which names the population size among the few experimental choices that most distort a comparison~\cite{9,10}.

We report that in a widely used platform this controlled quantity is not, in general, what it appears to be, and we argue that the underlying failure mode is general enough to deserve a framework. In PlatEMO~\cite{11}, a reference-vector set is obtained by calling a constructor that returns both the vectors and their number. A large number of algorithm files pass the user's population size to that constructor and then assign the second output back to the population size, so that the requested size is silently replaced by the size of the reference lattice. The constructor does not return the requested size: over the range $2\le M\le 15$, $M\le N\le 1000$, it never returns more points than requested and returns exactly the requested number in only \retExactFrac\% of cases over that grid (about 1.6\% at $M\ge3$, the regime in which these methods run). At $N=100$ the platform therefore runs with \retMthree{} individuals at three objectives and \retMten{} at ten objectives, a reduction of \retMthreeDef\% and \retMtenDef\% with no message to the user. The same mechanism can be present in any library that returns a reference set together with a size that is then written into a shared problem object.

Three quantities have to be kept apart, because the whole failure lives in the gap between them: the size a study requests and declares, $N_{\mathrm{req}}$; the size the method actually evaluates, $N_{\mathrm{real}}$; and the size the run records, $N_{\mathrm{rep}}$. We traced all three in the platform studied here (Table~S9), and the outcome is unambiguous: the constructor returns 91 for a request of 100, and nine of the thirteen affected algorithms then evaluate exactly those 91 individuals, so for those nine $N_{\mathrm{real}} = N_{\mathrm{rep}} = \retMthree{}$ while $N_{\mathrm{req}} = 100$; the other four evaluate fewer. The platform always records the constructor's cardinality --- never the request, which the assignment has overwritten --- so for the four algorithms that shrink their own reference set during the search it does not record the size actually used either, and the recorded figure overstates the population. What is missing throughout is any signal that the request was not honoured, and the consequence is a confounded comparison rather than a corrupted result. In the same release, NSGA-II, SPEA2 and IBEA contain no such assignment and run at exactly 100, so a table that reports $N=100$ while mixing NSGA-III with NSGA-II, or MOEA/D with SPEA2, or RVEA with IBEA, would be comparing 91, 83, 80, 66 or 31 with 100. The nominal size in the table is the same; the runs are not.

That is the failure the calculus addresses, and it is a reporting failure rather than a coding mistake. The rounding itself is unavoidable: a symmetric lattice in $M$ objectives admits only certain cardinalities, so a request that falls between two admissible values has to be rounded. What is avoidable is that the rounded value replaces the requested one without the study being told, so that the number printed in the experimental section is no longer the number the run used. It follows that the remedy is not to forbid the write-back, which many algorithms use deliberately to keep the population and the reference set consistent, but to make the divergence visible and to report the size that was realised.

The size actually realised leaves traces that a reader can check without instrumenting the platform, which is what makes the problem detectable rather than merely suspected. The per-generation cost of a reference-vector method is proportional to $N_{\mathrm{real}}$, so under a fixed evaluation budget the number of completed generations is inversely proportional to it; in our controlled runs the declared size of 100 corresponds to \genFix{} generations and the realised size of 91 to \genHost{}, a ratio of \genRatio{} against the predicted \popRatio{}. A study that reports its generation count, or publishes convergence curves against generations, therefore exposes the discrepancy, and a study that reports only the final indicator hides it.

The mechanism is easy to miss: it surfaced in our own work only when an adaptive scheduling layer refused to reproduce, and most of its apparent gain disappeared once the population size was matched.

We therefore present the episode not as a bug report but as the calculus it motivates; the four parts are stated as (C1)-(C4) below and developed in Section~\ref{sec:framework}.

The contribution is therefore not the disclosure of a defect but the construction and validation of an instrument, in four parts. (C1)~A \emph{fidelity calculus}: Definition~\ref{def:fidelity} separates the requested, realised and reported sizes, Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} characterises the admissible cardinalities exactly and shows the induced deficit is one-signed, and Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} fixes when cardinality alone can favour an arm: only when the compared output sets are nested, since two independent runs need not be. (C2)~A \emph{decision procedure}: infidelity is reduced to a syntactic pattern decidable over a source tree, with its soundness stated and its residual measured against fourteen parser constructions. (C3)~A \emph{repair catalogue}: the admissible repair is shown to be algorithm-dependent, with three cases demonstrated and a fourth diagnosed, and the mechanism of the hardest case measured rather than asserted. (C4)~\emph{Validation}: a census of the distribution, a controlled fifteen-problem study with a paired design and an indicator-cardinality control, and a reporting protocol with fully released materials.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
Four strands of recent work frame this study. The first is the reference-vector family itself. Reference-vector guided selection remains the workhorse for many-objective problems, and recent methods continue to build on it, for example by adapting the reference vectors to sparse or irregular fronts and by replacing the convergence measure used inside selection~\cite{13}. The family keeps expanding within the community, most recently with a unified dominance--decomposition framework proposed for feature selection~\cite{14}. In all of these methods the population size is the hidden constant, because the reference set and the population are indexed by the same $N$.

The second strand is the benchmarking-methodology literature. Community guidance has long warned that an indicator value is comparable only when the experimental protocol is fixed~\cite{15,16}, and comparison results have since been shown to depend strongly on the specification of the population size, the quantity we audit~\cite{9}; wall-clock time rather than evaluations alone has been argued to be the budget that matters in practice~\cite{17}; the reproducibility of evolutionary computation has been examined in its own right~\cite{12}; the rigour of the field has been critically reviewed~\cite{18}; and the design of benchmarks with practical impact has been reconsidered~\cite{19}. The closest antecedent to our work is an analysis of single-objective algorithms implemented across thirteen frameworks, which found that even identical parameter settings can yield different results because of implementation differences; that study raises the general concern and calls for guidelines, but it does not isolate a mechanism that a reader can check, and it covers single-objective algorithms~\cite{10}. Our contribution is a mechanism of exactly that kind, located in the reference-vector constructor, together with the audit and the repairs that make it actionable. The journal has itself published work of this kind~\cite{34}, the genre this paper belongs to.

The third strand is the arrival of methods driven by large language models, which generate offspring and steer multi-objective search end to end~\cite{4,5,6,20}; they are benchmarked against reference-vector baselines drawn from the same platforms, so a systematic mismatch in those baselines propagates into the comparisons that establish their gains.

The fourth strand widens the problem classes to which reference-vector methods are applied --- surrogate-assisted and constrained variants~\cite{21,22,23}, with applications including work published in this journal~\cite{24,25}. Every such family inherits the reference-vector machinery and with it the population-size convention under audit, so the problem is tied not to one benchmark but to the substrate of a class of methods.

Two positions come closest to ours and deserve an explicit contrast, because the difference is that between a premise and an instrument. Ishibuchi \emph{et al.}~\cite{9} show that the population size must be specified and held fixed for a fair comparison; we show \emph{how} a specified size can nevertheless stop being the size that is used, formalise the failure (Definition~\ref{def:fidelity} and Propositions~1--2), and turn it into an audit, a repair catalogue and a reporting protocol. Ravber \emph{et al.}~\cite{10} show that implementation differences across frameworks change single-objective results and call for guidelines; we locate one such mechanism in the reference-vector constructor of a multi-objective platform, expose its size effect with the reference-set cardinality held at $L$ together with the residual between two same-size arms through a three-arm design, and quantify the consequence rather than only raising the concern.

\section{A fidelity calculus}
\label{sec:framework}
We now state the calculus in a form that is independent of any particular library. Throughout, a \emph{reference-vector method} is an algorithm whose population is organised around a finite set of reference vectors, the symbol $N$ denotes the population size that the user sets, and $M$ denotes the number of objectives. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarises where the three quantities come from and where fidelity is checked.

\subsection{The fidelity principle}
\label{sec:principle}
We first make the notion precise, because the whole framework rests on distinguishing three quantities that are usually conflated.

\begin{definition}[Population-size fidelity]\label{def:fidelity}
Consider a reference-vector method configured with a requested size $N_{\mathrm{req}}$. At a checkpoint --- the final result set, after any reference-set adaptation --- the method holds a population $P = \bigcup_k P_k$ of total size $N_{\mathrm{real}} = \sum_k |P_k|$, and its output records a size $N_{\mathrm{rep}}$. The method is \emph{population-size faithful} if $N_{\mathrm{req}} = N_{\mathrm{real}} = N_{\mathrm{rep}}$ at that checkpoint; otherwise it suffers a \emph{size infidelity}. Two drifts are worth separating. The \emph{structural drift} $\delta_C = N_{\mathrm{req}} - L$ is the shortfall between the request and the cardinality $L$ the reference-vector constructor returns; Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} shows it is non-negative. The \emph{runtime drift} $\delta_R = N_{\mathrm{req}} - N_{\mathrm{real}}$ is the shortfall between the request and the population actually evaluated at the checkpoint; its sign is not fixed in general, and a method that prunes its own reference set after construction realises fewer individuals than the constructor returned (so $\delta_R > \delta_C$). Both are non-negative throughout the platform studied here, which is an observation about this release rather than a general property: a method that pads or reseeds its reference set can make either drift negative.
\end{definition}

The definition isolates the three quantities deliberately, because a run can satisfy any two while violating the third. Three variants of the failure are worth separating. A run that rounds its request and then records the rounded value is honest about what it did: it is unfaithful only in the sense that the request could not be honoured, and the discrepancy is found by comparing $N_{\mathrm{req}}$ with $N_{\mathrm{real}}$ before the run rather than after it. A run that rounds, records the rounded value faithfully, and is then written up in a study whose tables state the original request is the case that threatens a comparison; there the platform is right and the paper is not, and the discrepancy is found by comparing the number in the table with the number the run realised. A run whose population changes again after the constructor has returned --- because the algorithm prunes its own reference set --- leaves a record that matches neither the request nor the population, and then the platform itself overstates the run. Section~\ref{sec:case} shows that PlatEMO produces the second variant for all affected algorithms and the third variant as well for four of them. All three quantities are observable without modifying the method or the library: $N_{\mathrm{req}}$ is what the study configures, $N_{\mathrm{real}}$ follows from the cardinality of the reference set and the evaluation cost of a generation, and $N_{\mathrm{rep}}$ is what the run stores. This is why an audit can rest on the definition rather than on the intent of the code.

Fidelity matters for three reasons. First, the realised population fixes the cardinality of the reference set and hence the resolution of the search. Second, it fixes the evaluation budget consumed per generation and therefore the number of generations that fit into a fixed budget. Third, the reported size is the quantity a reader uses to judge whether two methods were compared under the same conditions, and this third reason is the most insidious, because an unfaithful run still produces a valid Pareto approximation and raises no error.

It is useful to separate fidelity from quality: an unfaithful run is not necessarily a poor run, and a faithful run is not necessarily a good one. Fidelity is the weaker property that the experiment is what it claims to be --- exactly the property that a comparison across methods presupposes. It follows that restoring fidelity does not in itself improve an algorithm: it changes what the reported number means. The empirical sections below are written with this distinction in view, and we therefore report the direction and the size of every difference rather than treating fidelity as a performance claim.

The definition's practical interest comes from two facts that convert an anecdote into a checkable statement.

\begin{proposition}[One-sided structural drift]\label{prop:exact}
The constructor used in this study (Section~\ref{sec:case}) is the two-layer simplex lattice: a first layer of $\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}$ points, with $H_1$ the smallest integer satisfying $\binom{H_1+M}{M-1}>N$, and, when $H_1<M$, a second layer of $\binom{H_2+M-1}{M-1}$ points with $H_2$ the smallest \emph{non-negative} integer satisfying $\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}+\binom{H_2+M}{M-1}>N$, added only when $H_2\ge1$; the second layer is the $H_2$-division simplex lattice scaled by $1/2$ and shifted by $1/(2M)$ in every coordinate (``$W_2/2+1/(2M)$''), which is what prevents its points from duplicating the boundary directions. Then $L(N,M)=\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}+\binom{H_2+M-1}{M-1}\le N$ for all $M\ge2$, $N\ge M$; the structural drift $\delta_C = N - L(N,M)$ is therefore non-negative and can only remove individuals, never add them.
\end{proposition}

\proof If $H_2=0$ the second layer is absent and $L=\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}$. By the choice of $H_1$, $H_1-1$ satisfies $\binom{(H_1-1)+M}{M-1}\le N$, and $\binom{(H_1-1)+M}{M-1}=\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}$, so $L\le N$; the boundary $M=N$ falls here with $H_1=1$, where the layer contributes $\binom{M}{M-1}=M=N$ rows. If $H_2\ge1$, the choice of $H_2$ gives that $H_2-1$ satisfies $\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}+\binom{(H_2-1)+M}{M-1}\le N$, and $\binom{(H_2-1)+M}{M-1}=\binom{H_2+M-1}{M-1}$ is exactly the number of rows the second layer contributes, so $L=\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}+\binom{H_2+M-1}{M-1}\le N$. A duplicated direction between the two layers would not change the row count that $L(N,M)$ measures, so it cannot affect the inequality. The claim that the two layers never yield a duplicated direction, stated over the scanned range, is a separate empirical check, not a premise of this proof.

Equality $L(N,M)=N$ holds exactly when the request coincides with a cardinality the constructor can produce --- $\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}$ with $H_2=0$, or $\binom{H_1+M-1}{M-1}+\binom{H_2+M-1}{M-1}$ with $H_2\ge1$ --- which we call an \emph{admissible} size for this constructor.

We verified the proposition exhaustively rather than trusting the argument alone. Over $2\le M\le 15$ and $M\le N\le 1000$, that is \retCombos{} distinct combinations, the realised count exceeds the request in \retExceed{} cases, and the two layers never yield a duplicated direction. The counts were also checked against the platform's own constructor at \nProbedDirect{} points spanning the range and at all \nGridCells{} cells of the grid reported in Table~S10, with no disagreement; as regression anchors, $L(4,3)=3$ (no second layer is added) and $L(500,9)=495$ (the first layer alone exhausts the request), which pin down the $H_2=0$ branch that the proof handles separately.

The range is the range we scanned, not a documented limit: the platform's problems take the number of objectives as an argument, and its sources carry defaults up to ten (for example, the MaF8 problem declares \texttt{obj.M~=~10} as its default). The scan extends to $M=15$, half as much again beyond that default ceiling, which comfortably brackets the objective counts at which reference-vector methods are actually run and is cheap enough to enumerate completely; beyond it the closed form still applies at negligible cost. The complete table $L(N,M)$ over that range is released as a machine-readable file, and points outside it follow from the closed form of the constructor rather than from a measurement.

\begin{proposition}[Generation inflation]\label{prop:gen}
Consider a method that states a size $N_{\mathrm{req}}=N$ but evaluates a population of size $N_{\mathrm{real}}=L<N$ and consumes $cL$ evaluations per generation, stopping when the budget $\mathrm{FE}_{\max}$ is exhausted. If $cN\le \mathrm{FE}_{\max}$, the ratio of the generations the actual run completes to those the stated size implies is $\big\lfloor \mathrm{FE}_{\max}/(cL)\big\rfloor / \big\lfloor \mathrm{FE}_{\max}/(cN)\big\rfloor$, which converges to $N/L$ as $\mathrm{FE}_{\max}\to\infty$; the relative excess therefore approaches $(N-L)/L$, with a floor-induced error of order $O(cN/\mathrm{FE}_{\max})$.
\end{proposition}

\proof The number of generations is the budget divided by the per-generation cost $cN_{\mathrm{real}}$. With $N_{\mathrm{real}}=L$ the run completes $\lfloor \mathrm{FE}_{\max}/(cL)\rfloor$ generations, whereas the stated size implies $\lfloor \mathrm{FE}_{\max}/(cN)\rfloor$. Writing $x=\mathrm{FE}_{\max}/c$, the ratio is $\lfloor x/L\rfloor / \lfloor x/N\rfloor$, which tends to $(x/L)/(x/N)=N/L$ as $x\to\infty$; replacing each floor by its argument incurs an error of at most $O(cN/\mathrm{FE}_{\max})$.

The premise of Proposition~\ref{prop:gen} is the reporting condition and not the internal mechanics: the excess appears whenever the quantity a study states is $N_{\mathrm{req}}$ while the quantity the run realises is $L$. A run that both realises $L$ and reports $L$ is described by the same formula with $N=L$ and shows no excess, which is the consistency condition of the framework. Two consequences follow. When the constructor is the one of Section~\ref{sec:case}, Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} makes the drift one-signed, so the structural deficit is one-signed rather than randomly perturbed. When the comparison is made under a fixed evaluation budget, Proposition~\ref{prop:gen} makes the drift translate directly into a difference in the number of generations, which is exactly the quantity that a convergence curve displays. Neither fact is visible from a single reported population size, which is why the framework is needed.

\subsection{Cardinality and the sign of the induced bias}
\label{sec:bias}
A comparison between two arms that differ in how many individuals they evaluate is not neutral, and when the compared output sets are nested the direction is exact. Let $R$ denote the reference set against which the indicator is computed and, in the normalised objective space, $\mathrm{IGD}(\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r\in R}\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\lVert r-p\rVert$.

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity under inclusion]\label{prop:bias}
Fix the reference set $R$ and let $\mathcal{P}$ be any population. For any further set $Q$, $\mathrm{IGD}(\mathcal{P}\cup Q)\le\mathrm{IGD}(\mathcal{P})$ and $\mathrm{HV}(\mathcal{P}\cup Q)\ge\mathrm{HV}(\mathcal{P})$. Consequently, if one arm's output set contains the other's --- as when the same run is read at $k$ and at $k+m$ points --- the arm with more points is weakly favoured by both indicators.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every term of the sum is a minimum over a set that contains the original one, so no term increases; the dominated region of a union contains the dominated region of either part.
\end{proof}

The proposition is deliberately narrow. Two independent runs do not stand in an inclusion relation --- a larger population may search worse --- so what it fixes is the direction cardinality alone produces when the comparison is nested, not a universal bias; across independent runs we observe no uniform sign (Section~\ref{sec:across}). This is why the comparison below uses unpaired tests rather than arguing from the proposition.

\begin{proposition}[The generation count as a consistency check]\label{prop:ident}
If each generation costs $c\,N_{\mathrm{real}}$ evaluations with $c$ approximately constant across the settings compared, then $g\,N_{\mathrm{real}}\approx B/c$ for every run of a fixed budget $B$. The product of the reported generation count and the declared size is therefore comparable across arms, and a mismatch larger than the observed drift in $c$ indicates that the arms did not both run at the size they declare.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A run of $g$ generations at $cN_{\mathrm{real}}$ evaluations each consumes $g\,c\,N_{\mathrm{real}}=B$. The per-individual cost is not exactly constant --- we measure $1.99$ against $2.00$ --- so the relation is a check to within that drift rather than an identity, and we use it as such.
\end{proof}

The two arms of Table~\ref{tab:cost} satisfy the check to within the drift: $552\times91=50{,}232$ and $499\times100=49{,}900$, a difference of 0.7\%, so a reader given only the declared size and the generation count can already see that the two rows did not run at the same size.

\subsection{A decision procedure for size infidelity}
\begin{definition}[Constructor write-back]\label{def:pattern}
A statement is a \emph{constructor write-back} if it assigns to the field of the problem object that stores the population size a value that is the cardinality returned by a call to a reference-vector constructor. A file is \emph{lexically infidelic} if its token stream contains a constructor write-back and no later statement in the same file reconciles the assigned value with the request. The audit decides the lexical subclass in which the call and the assignment occur in a single statement and no alias or dynamic dispatch intervenes.
\end{definition}

\label{sec:audit-proc}
The audit rests on a single observation: size infidelity arises when the output of a reference-vector constructor is written into a shared object that stores the population size. Algorithm~\ref{alg:audit} formalises the procedure. The audit is a lexical procedure: the statement set $S$ is the sequence of statements that the tokeniser yields for every file it accepts, and no scope or alias resolution is attempted; files the tokeniser cannot read are counted and reported rather than skipped silently, and calls the pattern cannot resolve --- an alias, a handle, or dynamic dispatch --- are counted as unresolved and are the source of the false negatives measured below. Over the audited tree every file is read, so $S$ is the whole distribution and the unresolved calls are the only residual.

\begin{algorithm}
\footnotesize
%\floatname{algorithm}{Algorithm}
\caption{Auditing a library for population-size infidelity}
\label{alg:audit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE source tree $\mathcal{T}$; constructor set $\mathcal{C}$ with documented ranges $R_\phi$ of $(N,M)$
    \ENSURE census of affected call sites, files and algorithms; the deficit function $\delta_C(N,M)$
    \STATE $S \gets$ calls to any $\phi \in \mathcal{C}$ whose return list contains the population-size field
    \FOR{each call $s \in S$}
        \IF{the pair $(N,M)$ at $s$ is not matched by the literal token pattern}
            \STATE mark $s$ \emph{unresolved}; continue
        \ENDIF
        \STATE realised deficit $\delta_s \gets N - L_\phi(N,M_s)$ at the bound value $(N,M_s)$
        \STATE range deficit $\Delta_s \gets \max_{(N,M)\in R_\phi}\big(N - L_\phi(N,M)\big)$
        \IF{$\delta_s = 0$}
            \STATE classify $s$ as faithful; continue
        \ENDIF
        \STATE record $s$, its file and its algorithm directory, with $\delta_s$ and $\Delta_s$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE return the census grouped by algorithm family, together with the deficit function $\delta_C$
\STATE (run-level check) for each algorithm, compare the final $|P|$ with $N_{\mathrm{rep}}$, and report any post-construction shrink
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The only judgement the audit requires is the token-pattern test, and the deficit evaluation uses only the documented behaviour of the constructor rather than the code that calls it, so the census is reproducible by a reader who has the same release. For the class of Definition~\ref{def:pattern} the procedure is \emph{sound} by construction: it flags a file only when a token pattern matches an assignment of the constructor's size output to the field that stores the population size, so every flagged file contains the assignment. It is not complete for programs that reach the same effect through an alias or dynamic dispatch, and we measure that residual rather than assert its absence: across fourteen parser constructions, twelve are handled as intended and two dynamic ones are reported as false negatives, with no parse failures over the audited tree. Its cost is linear: each file is parsed once and each statement is tested against a constant-size pattern, so the audit runs in $O(FS)$ time over $F$ files of $S$ statements and needs $O(S)$ memory for one file's token sequence, which is why the whole distribution is audited in seconds. In the case study of Section~\ref{sec:case} the constructor is a lattice whose cardinality is the largest admissible value not exceeding the request, which makes $\delta_C$ a deterministic function that can be tabulated once and reused; by Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} the sign of $\delta_C$ is non-negative throughout.

Two implementation choices decide whether the census is trustworthy, and we make both explicit because a naive search over source text is not. First, the match must not be satisfied by a comment or by a string literal, since algorithm files document the pattern in comments and build expressions with \texttt{eval} and \texttt{sprintf}. We therefore run the pattern over a lexically reduced form of each file in which line comments, block comments and single- and double-quoted literals have been replaced by blanks, with line and column positions preserved, and we additionally require the hit to sit on a live statement. This is a lexical and not a syntactic analysis, so it can in principle miss a call assembled dynamically. We therefore wrote a suite of fourteen positive and negative constructions, covering the constructions a MATLAB source file can use to hide such a call: comments, block comments, both quote styles, escaped quotes, a percent sign inside a string, a transpose before the match, a line continuation, an assignment to another field, and calls assembled by \texttt{eval} and by \texttt{sprintf}. Twelve behave as intended and the two dynamic constructions cannot be seen lexically at all; they are disclosed as limitations. The suite earned its keep: it showed that the parser initially missed a call split across a line continuation, which we fixed, and the census is unchanged by that fix, so the gap was a false-negative path rather than a miscount. Because a miss under-counts, we treat the census as a lower bound, and we inspected the \nLexFiles{} surviving hits by hand. The parser and the suite ship with the release.

Second, the audit records three quantities and not one: the requested size, the cardinality the constructor returns, and the population the run actually evaluates. Recording only the first would miss the drift, and recording only the first two would miss the four algorithms of Section~\ref{sec:case} whose population changes again after the constructor has returned.

\subsection{A repair catalogue}
\label{sec:catalogue}
Restoring fidelity is not a single edit, because algorithms couple their population to their reference set in different ways. We distinguish four cases, and for each we state the edit, the constructor requirement and the test that fails before the edit and passes after it.

\emph{Case A: directions only.} The reference vectors are used only to define directions, and the number of individuals need not equal the number of vectors. The edit discards the second return value of the constructor, \texttt{[Z,\textasciitilde]~=~UniformPoint(N,M);}, so that the reference set is left untouched while the population keeps the requested size. The applicable test is that \texttt{size(Population,1)} equals the requested $N$ at the end of the first generation while the reference set has $L<N$ rows.

\emph{Case B: explicit indexing.} The algorithm indexes its individuals by reference vector, so the population must equal the reference set. Discarding the size output breaks the indexing, and the run fails with an index-out-of-bounds error, which is the test for the case; the constructor must instead be replaced by one that returns exactly $N$ points. A minimal exactly-$N$ constructor is a lattice of the largest admissible size below $N$, padded with the $N-L$ points that maximise the minimum distance to the points already chosen, with a de-duplication step and an assertion that the number of rows equals $N$; because the padding is added after the lattice, the reference set changes as well as the population, which is why this repair is not interchangeable with case~A.

\emph{Case C: normalisation by inter-vector geometry.} The algorithm penalises a candidate by the smallest angle between neighbouring reference vectors. Here neither previous repair is admissible without additional care, and the reason can be measured. Table~\ref{tab:angle} reports the smallest pairwise angle of the two constructors. The exactly-$N$ constructor does not collapse the angle to zero, but it reduces it, by a factor of \angRatioThree{} at $N=100$, $M=3$ (\angNbiThree$^\circ$ to \angMudThree$^\circ$) and by a factor of \angRatioFive{} at $M=5$ (\angNbiFive$^\circ$ to \angMudFive$^\circ$). In RVEA the selection metric is the angle-penalised distance $\mathrm{APD}(F_i,V_j)=\|F_i\|\big(1+M(\theta_{i,j}/\gamma_j)^{\alpha}\big)$~\cite{3}, in which the inter-vector angle $\gamma_j$ enters the denominator of the \emph{angular penalty}, not of the convergence term $\|F_i\|$: a smaller $\gamma_j$ therefore amplifies the angular penalty and suppresses the convergence pressure along that reference vector. This is a measured candidate mechanism rather than a claim of sole cause, since the added points also change the search dynamics. A repair for this case nonetheless has to bound the angle explicitly rather than merely return $N$ points. A minimal admissible construction is therefore: take the largest admissible lattice $L\le N$, then add the $N-L$ remaining directions one at a time, each chosen to maximise the smallest angle to the directions already present, and reject a candidate whose inclusion would drop that angle below a floor $\theta_{\min}=1^\circ$; if the floor cannot be met, fall back to $N=L$ and report the fallback rather than silently shrinking the population.

We implemented this construction and measured it. At $N=100$ with three, five and eight objectives it returns exactly 100 directions while retaining the smallest angle of the underlying lattice ($5.19^\circ$, $18.19^\circ$, $16.38^\circ$), where the plain exactly-$N$ constructor of Table~\ref{tab:angle} degrades them to $3.57^\circ$, $7.89^\circ$ and $13.11^\circ$; the floor was never binding on the settings studied, so the fallback never fired (Table~S4 records the three settings tested); the floor is a safety check set far below the angles that occur in practice, so its exact value does not drive the results. The applicable test is therefore two-sided and both halves are met: the minimum pairwise angle of the repaired set is at least $\theta_{\min}$, and the cardinality equals the requested $N$.

\emph{Case D: post-construction shrink.} When an algorithm prunes or re-adapts its reference set after construction (RVEA, DGEA, LMOCSO, RVEAa in Section~\ref{sec:case}), no constructor-level repair can restore fidelity; the repair is to report the final reference-set cardinality and re-index the population to it after the last adaptation, and the test is that the recorded size equals the final population size at the checkpoint of Definition~\ref{def:fidelity}.

The catalogue is the reason a framework is needed at all: a reader who adopts ``the one-line repair'' as a universal rule will silently break the algorithms in cases B and C, and one who adopts the exactly-$N$ constructor as a universal rule will reduce the angular resolution of those in case C by up to a factor of \angRatioFive{}. Section~\ref{sec:across} exhibits three of the four cases (A--C) on real algorithms; case~D is diagnosed from the trace of Table~S9.

\subsection{A reporting protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Finally, the framework prescribes what a study should report --- a schema rather than advice, because advice is not adoptable. A reference-vector study should print, for every configuration it compares, the seven columns

\begin{center}
\texttt{N\_req} $\mid$ \texttt{N\_real} $\mid$ \texttt{N\_rep} $\mid$ \texttt{constructor} $\mid$ \texttt{repair} $\mid$ \texttt{generations} $\mid$ \texttt{FE\_used} ,
\end{center}

with three decision rules attached, and with the check expressed as code so that it can be run rather than remembered. Rule~1: if $N_{\mathrm{req}} \neq N_{\mathrm{real}}$, the row must say so, and any claim about the method must use $N_{\mathrm{real}}$. Rule~2: if $N_{\mathrm{rep}} \neq N_{\mathrm{real}}$, the platform's record is not the population that was used, so the row must report all three quantities and no downstream claim may rely on the record; Table~S9 shows that four of the sixteen traced algorithms are in this state. Rule~3: a comparison table must not mix rows whose \texttt{N\_real} differ unless the difference is the object of study, which Table~S9 shows is violated by default. The protocol costs one line in the experimental section and one assertion at the end of a run, \texttt{assert(numel(Population) == N\_real \&\& Problem.N == N\_real)}. The protocol deliberately does not require suppressing the write-back of the size into the shared problem object, which many algorithms use to keep the population and the reference set consistent. What it requires is that the number in the table be the number the run used, and that an unrealisable request be declared as such before the runs rather than discovered afterwards. A study that cannot realise a request should either move the request to the nearest admissible value, as we do for the declared arm of Section~\ref{sec:exp}, or state the realised value and treat the request as nominal.

\section{Validation on a widely used platform}
\label{sec:case}
\subsection{The override}
We audit PlatEMO 4.16, released in July 2026 with 360 algorithms and 630 problems --- the version that added the host of Section~\ref{sec:exp}, and the reference point for the census below. The affected line is
\begin{center}
\texttt{[Z,Problem.N] = UniformPoint(Problem.N,Problem.M);}
\end{center}
in which the second output of the constructor~\cite{26} is written into the population size. The constructor is the simplex lattice of Das and Dennis~\cite{27} extended to a second, shifted inner layer, as introduced by the reference-point generation of NSGA-III~\cite{1}; it caps the total cardinality at the request, which is an implementation choice rather than a mathematical necessity, and the second layer is itself a fidelity effort that shrinks the gap between request and realisation. What we examine is the write-back: the capped value is assigned to the field that stores the user's request. Table~S9 traces the three quantities for \nTraced{} reference-vector algorithms at a requested size of 100 and three objectives. Two facts follow. First, because \texttt{PROBLEM} is a handle class, the assignment mutates the caller's object, so the platform records the constructor's cardinality (91) and never the request; it records $L$ exactly --- faithful to the constructor, not to the request. Second, that recorded value is not always the population used: nine of the thirteen affected algorithms coincide at \retMthree{}, but RVEA finishes at 83 individuals, DGEA at 80, LMOCSO at 66 and RVEAa at \nRealMin{} (factors 1.10, 1.14, 1.38 and 2.94 over the recorded value), while NSGA-II, SPEA2 and IBEA are unaffected and run at the requested 100, so a study comparing any two at a nominal $N=100$ compares populations differing by up to a factor of 3.2.

\subsection{The same request in another library}
\label{sec:xplatform}
The framework claims to be portable, so we ran the audit on a second library rather than asserting that it would apply. pymoo is a widely used Python framework with a reference-direction module of its own, and it exposes the same two constructions: a Das--Dennis lattice and a factory that returns an exact number of directions. The audit's trigger, a routine that returns a reference set together with a size that is then written into a shared population-size field, is \emph{absent} there: the Das--Dennis entry point takes the partition count and derives the cardinality, and the exact-count factory is a separate call, so no user-set population size is overwritten. The audit therefore returns a negative result on pymoo \cite{28}, which is what makes it a test rather than a tautology.

The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:xplatform} is therefore not a second defect report. What is \emph{not} absent is the underlying arithmetic, and the difference between the two libraries is instructive: the table compares the realised cardinality for the same nominal request. At three objectives both libraries return 91, because the lattice is the same; at five, eight and ten objectives they diverge, by factors of \xRatioFive{}, \xRatioEight{} and 1.18. At eight objectives a study that declares $N=100$ realises \nPlatMeight{} directions in PlatEMO and \nPymoMeight{} in pymoo, a factor of \xRatioEight{}. The divergence is not a defect of either library: PlatEMO returns a two-layer lattice that caps the total at the request, while pymoo selects the largest single-layer lattice whose cardinality does not exceed the request and stops there; the two constructions differ, so the ratio reflects that difference, not a shortcoming of either. The negative result on pymoo concerns the \emph{idiom} --- the write-back of a size alongside a reference set --- being absent, not the absence of infidelity of any kind. The point for the framework is that the nominal size does not determine the realised size even across two libraries, let alone across algorithms within one library, and that a comparison table reporting only the nominal size is therefore unverifiable by construction.

\section{Empirical consequences of the override}
\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
The host is FDSEA~\cite{29}, a recent large-scale reference-vector method that contains the assignment and couples its population to the reference set only through the directions, so it is case~A of Section~\ref{sec:catalogue}. Its loop is governed by three constants ($n_{\mathrm{ex}}$, $\gamma$ and $K$); we hold $K$ at 5 and set the other two statically, so that the effect of the population size is separated from that of the constants, and the arm labelled ``published'' means the published constants held static rather than a literal re-run of the online update rule. We compare nine configurations of the same host, single-threaded, with budget $\budget$ and seeds $1,\dots,\seeds$ for the $D=300$ problems and \seedsExt{} for the $M=5$ and $D=1000$ problems: the host as published; repair~1; repair~2; an adaptive layer used only as a case study; and five static settings. The suite is \nProbs{} problems: DTLZ1, DTLZ2 and DTLZ7~\cite{30} with LSMOP1--LSMOP9~\cite{31} at $M=3$, $D=300$, plus LSMOP1 and LSMOP6 at $M=5$ and LSMOP1 at $D=1000$. The indicator is the inverted generational distance~\cite{32} to a \refFrontPts{}-point discretisation of the true front, which spans the unit interval in each objective, so the usual normalisation is the identity.

Two points about the design determine how the results are to be read. First, the comparison isolates something narrower than the framework as a whole. All nine configurations are launched with the same requested size $N=100$, and Section~\ref{sec:abc} adds a further declared arm that exposes the size effect with the reference-set cardinality held at $L$ together with the residual between two same-size arms; ``as published'' realises $N_{\mathrm{real}}=91$ because the override takes effect, and ``repair~1'' realises $N_{\mathrm{real}}=100$. The measured difference is therefore the difference between the declared condition and the realised one, and by construction it coincides with the difference between a 91-individual and a 100-individual run of the host. We do not claim that fidelity has an effect separable from the population size; we claim, and measure, that declaring one size while running another changes the conclusion a study would draw. Second, the termination test is evaluated at generation boundaries, so a run can overshoot the budget by less than one generation: the published arm consumes \feHost{} evaluations (\feOverPct\% above nominal) and the corrected arms \feFix{} exactly.

Both arms are launched from the same seed list, but same-seed runs are not bit-identical across configurations (Section~\ref{sec:abc}), so we do not assume pairing. A shared list does not make run $k$ of one arm the same trajectory as run $k$ of the other, because the configurations consume the generator differently, so we treat the runs of each arm as exchangeable draws and report the paired test only as a sensitivity check. The primary test is the two-sided Mann--Whitney test on the two independent samples, with the Holm--Bonferroni correction over the \nProbs{} comparisons; the paired signed-rank test is reported alongside for continuity with earlier analyses, and the sign test as a distribution-free summary. Throughout, the central tendency is reported as the \emph{median} over the runs, the interquartile range of the per-run record is used as spread, and every percentage change quoted in the text and in the tables is computed from medians as a ratio of medians, so that the two agree; optimisation runs occasionally produce a few dominating outliers, and the median is the robust summary. The raw per-run records are released, so the mean can be recovered exactly.

\subsection{Three arms: published, declared and repaired}
\label{sec:abc}
Because the published configuration differs from the repaired one in the population actually evaluated, a comparison between those two alone cannot say whether the problem is the declaration or the size. We therefore ran a third arm in which the host is configured at the size it will realise --- \retMthree{} at three objectives and \retMfive{} at five, which are the two values the constructor returns for a request of 100 at those objective counts (Table~S10) --- so that the declaration and the realisation agree. The three arms are then \emph{published} (declared 100, realised \retMthree{}), \emph{declared} (declared \retMthree{}, realised \retMthree{}) and \emph{repaired} (declared 100, realised 100). Table~\ref{tab:abc} gives all three pairwise comparisons, and each isolates a different thing: declared against published holds the realised size fixed at \retMthree{} and varies only the declaration, so it measures the residual between two same-size arms (the write-back is deterministic, so there is no declaration effect to measure); repaired against declared varies the realised size from \retMthree{} to 100 with the reference-set cardinality held at $L$, so it measures the \emph{size} effect; and repaired against published is their combination, which is the quantity a reader of the published paper actually experiences.

The declared arm requests \abcDeclReqMthree{} at three objectives and \abcDeclReqMfive{} at five --- the values the published arm realises --- so the two arms evaluate the same number of individuals, and over that grid the constructor returns the same lattice for either request. They nevertheless differ by a median of \abcDeclVsPubAbsMed\% (largest \abcDeclVsPubMax\%, Table~\ref{tab:abc}); we could not attribute that to a single mechanism from the released artefacts and do not present the arm as a manipulation check. We therefore treat the two same-size arms as independent replicates and compare arms with unpaired tests throughout, reporting the paired test only for continuity. Repaired and declared differ by a median of \abcRepVsDeclMed\%, which measures the population count together with the resulting 100-to-\retMthree{} ratio between population and reference set; we call it the size effect with that caveat.

\subsection{Effect of restoring fidelity on the host}
\label{sec:effect}
Table~\ref{tab:abc} reports the host on all \nProbs{} problems, Table~S3 of the supporting information adds repair~2, and Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows the per-problem median change. Restoring the requested size changes the median indicator from \fixMinGain\% to \fixMaxGain\%, the median change over the suite being \fixMedGain\%. The direction of the effect is not uniform, and we state the counts exactly rather than in summary. These counts are against the published arm --- the \emph{rep\,$-$\,pub} column of Table~\ref{tab:abc} --- which measures the combined (size plus residual) effect; the size effect (\emph{rep\,$-$\,decl}) is reported in Section~\ref{sec:abc}. The corrected host is no worse on \fixNoWorseN{} of the \nProbs{} problems and worse on the remaining \fixWorseN{}. We give the breakdown rather than a single count: twelve of the \fixNoWorseN{} improve in the same direction (eight of them above 1\%), LSMOP3 is a tie (the medians differ by about $3\times10^{-6}$, so the printed value is $+0.0\%$), and the two degradations are LSMOP2 and LSMOP7 (by $-1.5\%$ and $-4.1\%$ respectively). The size effect (\emph{rep\,$-$\,decl} in Table~\ref{tab:abc}, the population count changed while the reference set stays at its \retMthree{} directions) degrades three problems: LSMOP2, LSMOP4 and LSMOP7; in the combined count LSMOP4 instead improves by $+1.7\%$, because its positive residual ($+2.8\%$) offsets its negative size effect ($-1.2\%$). This is Proposition~\ref{prop:gen}'s fixed-budget trade-off in miniature: the extra nine individuals buy wider coverage and cost about a tenth of the generations, and on those three problems the lost generations outweigh the added coverage. Read as \emph{no worse}, the count is thirteen; read as \emph{improved}, it is twelve, and we state the count as no-worse because four of the twelve improvements are within 1\%.

Statistical significance is weaker than the direction count, and it depends on the test. Under the unpaired Mann--Whitney test the change is significant at the uncorrected $0.05$ level on \newUnpairedSig{} of the \nProbs{} problems and on \newUnpairedHolm{} after Holm--Bonferroni correction (DTLZ1, LSMOP8, LSMOP1 and LSMOP6 at five objectives); the paired signed-rank test, kept for continuity, separates \fixSigCount{} uncorrected and \fixSigHolm{} after correction. We lead with the unpaired result because the pairing assumption is not supported (Section~\ref{sec:abc}). Power over fifteen comparisons is limited, so we also report the win/loss counts as a distribution-free summary (Table~\ref{tab:sig}). A bootstrap interval for the median difference excludes zero on \ciExclN{} of the problems; being uncorrected, that count is exploratory and we do not rest a claim on it. The honest reading is that the direction is consistent across the suite and the magnitude is non-zero on about half the problems before correction and on four after it; Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} fixes the deficit, not its effect size.

Two points deserve emphasis. First, the measured difference is a size effect as much as a fidelity effect: the override makes the host run with fewer individuals than its reported size, so a study that prints $N=100$ and runs \retMthree{} individuals publishes a number that belongs to a smaller configuration. Because the inverted generational distance is non-increasing in the number of returned points, part of the gain is mechanical, and we separate it by downsampling each repaired front to the published arm's realised size (91 at three objectives, 85 at five) and recomputing the indicator against the same reference front. We draw random subsets rather than a coverage-maximising one, because a spread-maximising selection optimises the spacing of the selected points rather than the distance to the reference front that the indicator averages. Under the median of 200 random subsets (seed 0) the median gain falls from 1.7\% to 0.8\%, and the number of problems on which the repaired host is better falls from twelve to ten (Table~S8 gives the per-problem values). The surviving gains are concentrated on DTLZ1 (7.0\%), LSMOP1 (6.2\%), LSMOP6 at five objectives (7.0\%) and LSMOP1 at $D{=}1000$ (6.4\%). About half of the median gain is therefore mechanical, but a small genuine effect remains on ten of the fifteen problems. The direction is not an artefact of the indicator: the same comparison under the exact hypervolume, with normalised objectives and the reference point at 1.1, reproduces the sign on thirteen of the fifteen problems, the two exceptions being cases in which the hypervolume change is numerically zero (Table~S8). Second, the direction of the mean can disagree with the median and with the majority of runs when a few outliers dominate: on DTLZ7 the corrected host is better on \dtlzSevenWins{} of the \seeds{} runs and by median, yet its mean is worse --- which is why a single mean hides the effect.

\subsection{Two ways of restoring fidelity}
\label{sec:repairs}
Section~\ref{sec:abc} used the conservative repair to expose the size effect with the reference-set cardinality held at $L$. Table~S3 of the supporting information gives repair~2 alongside repair~1; the two are not equivalent, because repair~2 changes the reference set as well as the population, and on several problems the default constructor's smaller reference set is at least as good. Where the algorithm permits the population to differ from the reference set, repair~1 is the conservative choice.

\subsection{Consequence across algorithms}
\label{sec:across}
The affected set is not confined to one family, so a table that mixes affected and unaffected methods is confounded. Table~\ref{tab:peer} quantifies this on NSGA-III, and the picture there is more equivocal than for the host: the corrected NSGA-III is better on LSMOP1 but worse on LSMOP4 and LSMOP6, the paired signed-rank test, reported in the table as a sensitivity analysis, separates LSMOP2 and LSMOP4 even where the medians barely move because the override also changes the shape of the run-to-run distribution; we do not base a cross-algorithm significance claim on six problems for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:exp}. There is accordingly no uniform sign to the bias, which is the point of Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} being conditional.

We attempted the same repair on the other affected peers, where the catalogue of Section~\ref{sec:catalogue} becomes concrete. NSGA-III is case~A and accepts repair~1. MOEA/D is case~B: it pairs each weight vector with a neighbourhood over the reference set, so repair~1 fails with an index-out-of-bounds error while repair~2 restores it. RVEA and LMOCSO~\cite{33} are case~C: both normalise the angular penalty of the angle-penalised distance~\cite{3} by the smallest inter-vector angle, and the exactly-$N$ constructor reduces that angle by a factor of \angRatioThree{} at $M=3$ and \angRatioFive{} at $M=5$, so the penalty is amplified and the convergence pressure along that vector is suppressed --- the measured candidate mechanism in Table~\ref{tab:angle}, not the only one, since the added points also change the search dynamics. Repair~1 fails for RVEA too, later than for MOEA/D: it runs until the first reference-vector adaptation, at $f_r=0.1$ of the planned generations, where $V(1:N,:)$ needs at least $N$ rows. Table~S7 reports the catalogue validation on DTLZ1--7 at three objectives, and the per-seed records from which p-values can be computed are released.

On the degenerate fronts DTLZ5--6 the unbounded constructor degrades the Case~C pairs (RVEA's median IGD more than triples, LMOCSO's by over an order of magnitude), while the bounded-angle constructor repairs the damage --- for RVEA it is better than the published arm on all seven problems (Table~S7). The bounded-angle repair is validated on the DTLZ family; its behaviour on the large-scale LSMOP suite is left to the released scripts and to future work.

In the configurations tested, fidelity can be restored, but not uniformly: the admissible repair follows from the coupling between population and reference set, not from a universal rule, and the evidence is strongest for the host whose full fifteen-problem suite is reported, thinner for NSGA-III (six problems) and for the bounded-angle case on the DTLZ family only.

\subsection{Static settings and cost}
\label{sec:static}
Sweeping the two constants statically (Table~S1 of the supporting information) suggests that the exchange count is the load-bearing one, and it is the reference against which the adaptive layer is judged; both live in the supporting information. The host as published completes \genHost{} generations and the corrected host \genFix{}, a ratio of \genRatio{} against the ratio of realised population sizes, \popRatio{}, as Proposition~\ref{prop:gen} predicts in its asymptotic form. The account is not exact, and we state it rather than paper over it. Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives the raw figures, which close as $\mathrm{FE} = I + G\times g$, where $I$ is the one-time evaluation of the initial population ($91$ for the published arm, $100$ for the corrected arm) and $g$ the steady-state cost per generation: for the published arm $g=(\feHost{}-91)/\genHost{}\approx181.16$ and for the corrected arm $g=(\feFix{}-100)/\genFix{}\approx200.20$. The cost per individual is therefore $\approx1.99$ and $\approx2.00$ rather than a constant $2$, and this drift in the per-individual cost is what separates the observed ratio \genRatio{} from the predicted \popRatio{}; the \feOver{}-evaluation overshoot (\feOverPct\%) is only a small part of it. Because each arm has a single run, we present this as a consistency check of Proposition~\ref{prop:gen}, not a verification of it. The wall-clock differences follow from the same effect and are not evidence about the algorithm.

The same static family checks that the layer is not simply a badly chosen constant: against the best static setting it wins on only \layerBeatsStaticN{} of the \nProbs{} problems, and the exchange count is the constant that matters. Wall-clock differences follow the realised size and are not evidence about the algorithm.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have argued that the population size of a reference-vector MOEA is a controlled quantity that can silently stop being controlled, and we have given a calculus that makes the failure exact rather than anecdotal. It characterises the admissible cardinalities and the deficit they induce (Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}), fixes when cardinality alone favours an arm, namely under set inclusion rather than across independent runs (Proposition~\ref{prop:bias}), and provides a consistency check on the realised size from the reported generation count under a fixed budget (Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}) --- which is what lets a reader test a published table without instrumenting the platform. Applied to PlatEMO 4.16 the audit finds the assignment in at least \nFilesAffected{} of \nFilesTotal{} source files across at least \nAlgAffected{} of \nAlgTotal{} algorithms; on fifteen problems restoring the requested size leaves the raw-IGD median no worse on thirteen, better on ten after an indicator-cardinality control, with four Holm-corrected survivors under unpaired tests, and the exact hypervolume reproduces the sign on thirteen of the fifteen.

Two methodological conclusions follow. The first is that the calculus converts a diffuse worry about fairness into four checkable outputs: a census at the algorithm and file level, a deficit table, a repair catalogue with the mechanism of the hardest case measured, and a reporting protocol. The second is that the finding is a reporting concern, not a defect in the algorithms: rounding a simplex lattice is unavoidable and the write-back is often deliberate, while publishing a size the run did not use is avoidable. The trigger --- a constructor returning a reference set together with a size written into a shared field --- is not specific to PlatEMO, and the audit is stated language-neutrally, so any library with this pattern can be audited the same way.

The study has clear limits. The census measures one distribution at one version and is not a survey; we demonstrate the comparison risk in a controlled setting but do not survey the published literature, so how often published studies report the requested rather than the realised size remains open. The declared-versus-realised comparison coincides by construction with a difference in population size, so it is the indicator-cardinality control of Section~\ref{sec:effect} that separates the mechanical from the search component, and the residual effect it leaves is small. The $M=5$ and $D=1000$ cells use twenty seeds, and power is limited over fifteen comparisons. The framework detects and repairs infidelity but cannot decide how large a population should be.
